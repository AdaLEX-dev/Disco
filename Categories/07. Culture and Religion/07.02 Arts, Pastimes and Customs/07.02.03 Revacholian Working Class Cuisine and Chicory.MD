Title:
Revacholian Working Class Cuisine and Chicory

Text:
Revacholian Working Class Cuisine and the Culture of Chicory define the dietary staples, communal eating habits, and culinary survival strategies that have evolved across the impoverished districts of Revachol. Shaped by maritime geography, harsh coastal winters, and decades of chronic economic scarcity following the destruction of the Commune, the everyday diet of the Insulindian proletariat is characterized by heavy, starch rich ingredients, preserved seafood, and ingenious substitutes designed to maximize caloric endurance on a meager budget.

The foundational food staple of Revachol West is the ocean itself. Working class meals center around salted cod, pickled coastal herring, fried sprats, and hearty fish stews simmered with cabbage, root turnips, and stale rye bread. In Martinaise, dockworkers and coastal fishermen gather at communal street stalls and hostel lunch counters to consume hot meat pies, boiled potato dumplings, and pickled sausages drenched in sharp vinegar mustard. Fresh meat and imported vegetables remain rare luxuries, replaced by canned emergency rations and preserved foods that can withstand the damp humidity of unheated tenement flats.

No culinary element is more universally recognized across the city than chicory, the dark, bitter roasted root that serves as the ubiquitous working class substitute for unaffordable imported coffee beans. Brewed into an intensely astringent, jet black infusion over iron stove burners, chicory coffee is consumed by dockers, factory laborers, and militia detectives alike from early dawn to midnight. Sweetened with coarse brown beet sugar or diluted with sweetened condensed milk, chicory provides the essential, warm, bitter fuel that sustains the physical stamina of the Insulindian workforce against the pervasive coastal cold, morning fog, and dampness of the metropolis.
