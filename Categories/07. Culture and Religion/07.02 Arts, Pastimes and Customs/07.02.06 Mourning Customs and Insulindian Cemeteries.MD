Title:
Mourning Customs and Insulindian Cemeteries

Text:
Mourning Customs and Insulindian Cemeteries define the solemn funerary rituals, memorial architecture, and collective grieving practices that have taken root across the scarred landscape of Revachol. In a city where hundreds of thousands of lives were extinguished in the violent fires of the Commune and subsequent Coalition naval bombardments, death is not an occasional domestic tragedy, but a pervasive, architectural presence woven into the very fabric of everyday urban existence.

Funerary practices blend historic Dolorian religious traditions with secular working class memorial rites. Bereaved families observe protracted periods of mourning marked by the wearing of black armbands, the lighting of beeswax votive candles in drafty stone chapels, and the erection of spontaneous roadside shrines decorated with dried sea kelp, painted stones, and faded photographs of the deceased. In maritime districts like Martinaise, where the sea claims dockworkers and fishermen each winter, communities conduct solemn harbor vigils, casting wreaths of dried coastal heather into the freezing tides of the bay.

The cemeteries of Revachol stand as monumental landscapes of communal sorrow. Vast necropolises, such as the historic Cimetiere de la Paix in Old Town and the waterlogged cemetery on the western coastal dunes, are crowded with thousands of moss covered headstones, ornate family crypts, and mass trenches containing the unrecovered victims of Year '08. These sacred burial grounds are visited constantly by surviving relatives, elderly widows, and former comrades who tend to weathered graves with quiet, stubborn devotion. The cemeteries serve as sacred sanctuaries of remembrance where personal loss converges with the collective trauma of a shattered civilization, honoring those who perished while the living struggle on.
